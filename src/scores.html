Venom Hazard: how dangerous is the venom itself?

Two measurements from the lab: how toxic the venom is per milligram (LD50), and how much venom the snake can produce (venom yield). A snake needs both to be truly dangerous. Very toxic venom in tiny amounts, or huge amounts of mild venom, both score lower than a snake with a lot of potent venom.

LD50: how toxic the venom is

LD50 is the dose of venom that kills half of a group of test animals, usually mice. It is written in milligrams of venom per kilogram of body weight (mg/kg). The lower the number, the more toxic the venom.

When a headline calls a snake “the most venomous in the world”, it almost always means that snake has the lowest LD50.

What 0.025 mg/kg looks like

The inland taipan’s venom has an LD50 of 0.025 mg/kg when injected under the skin of mice (Broad, Sutherland & Coulter, 1979). For a typical 20-gram lab mouse, that works out to half a microgram.

0.025mg of venom per kg of mouse
× 0.020kg, a 20 g mouse
= 0.0005mg, or 0.5 micrograms: enough to kill half the mice in a group

We do not multiply this up to a “human lethal dose”. People are not large mice: body size does not scale toxicity in a straight line, and species respond differently to the same toxins.

How the test works

Researchers give several groups of mice increasing doses of venom and count how many die within a fixed time, commonly 24 or 48 hours. Plotted against dose, the results form an S-shaped curve. The LD50 is the dose where that curve crosses 50%.

The 50% point is calculated with statistical methods such as probit analysis, Spearman–Kärber or Reed–Muench, and a good study reports a confidence interval with it. Groups are often only 5 to 10 mice per dose, so that interval can be wide.

Why mice?

Mice are cheap, genetically consistent and well studied, which makes results comparable between labs. The test uses many animals, though, and toxinology is moving towards methods that use fewer (see “Other measures” below).

Where the venom goes changes the number

The same venom gives a different LD50 depending on how it is injected. This is the biggest source of confusion when figures from different studies are compared.

RouteInjected intoWhat it is used for
SCUnder the skinClosest to a real bite. Our preferred route.
IVA veinThe most standardised route; venom reaches the blood at once. Common in antivenom testing.
IPThe belly cavityHistorically the most common route because it is easy, but absorption is less predictable.
IMA muscleLess common; used in some tissue-damage studies.

Even the liquid the venom is dissolved in matters. In the same 1979 study, inland taipan venom measured 0.025 mg/kg in plain saline and 0.01 mg/kg when diluted with a protein carrier (bovine serum albumin). We use the saline figure and keep the other on record.

What LD50 is good for, and what it misses

Good for
  • One comparable number. It puts very different venoms on a single scale.
  • Testing antivenom. WHO guidelines measure antivenom strength by how many lethal doses of venom it neutralises.
  • Decades of data. Results going back to the 1970s can still be compared.
Misses
  • People are not mice. Mouse results do not reliably predict what a bite does to a person.
  • How much venom is delivered. LD50 is toxicity per milligram. That is why we add venom yield, below.
  • Injury that doesn’t kill. Tissue death, bleeding and lasting disability do not show up. Many viper bites maim far more often than they kill.
  • Slow killers. The test favours fast-acting nerve toxins and underrates venoms that kill over days through bleeding or kidney failure.
  • Natural variation. Venom changes with a snake’s age, region and diet, but studies often pool venom from several snakes.
  • How death happens. A single number says nothing about whether it was paralysis, bleeding or heart failure.

Venom yield: how much venom the snake has

Venom yield is the amount of venom collected when a snake is “milked”: it bites through a membrane stretched over a container, or its venom glands are gently pressed. The venom is freeze-dried and weighed, and the result is given in milligrams of dry venom.

Sources usually give a typical figure and a maximum. We score the maximum. Many older sources give only a range without saying what the lower number means, so the maximum is the more consistent figure to compare.

Dry, not wet

Fresh venom is mostly water, so wet-weight figures are several times higher than dry ones. We only score dry weights and keep wet figures on record, marked as not comparable.

What yield misses

  • Milked is not injected. Snakes control how much venom they use. Many defensive bites inject a fraction of the maximum, and some are “dry”, with no venom at all.
  • Big snakes, big yields. Yield rises with body size, so a record from one large captive snake can sit far above what a typical wild snake produces.
  • Thin evidence. Most yield figures come from older secondary compilations that don’t say how many snakes were milked. That is why most yields in our data are grade C.

How the two become one score

Worked through for Russell’s viper.

  1. Pick one LD50 per species

    Under-the-skin (SC) tests are preferred. Competing figures stay on record, and a species is flagged when they differ by more than 1.5×.

  2. Score potency from 0 to 100 on a log scale

    100 at the most toxic anchor (0.039 mg/kg), 0 at the least toxic (9.27 mg/kg). The anchors are the 2nd and 98th percentiles of all species, so one extreme value cannot stretch the scale. Anything beyond an anchor is capped.

  3. Score quantity from the maximum yield, also on a log scale

    From the smallest maximum yield in the dataset (0.7 mg) to the largest (1,102 mg).

  4. Average the two

    Potency and quantity count equally by default. Both are required: a species missing either gets no Venom Hazard.

Human Risk: how likely is a bite to happen and go badly?

Venom alone doesn’t decide how many people a snake harms. Human Risk combines four things about the snake and the people around it. Unlike the lab measurements, these are informed judgements, so treat the score as indicative.

Onset: why slow can score higher

You might expect the fastest-acting venoms to score highest. We score the other way round. A bite that seems mild at first often leads people to delay going to hospital, and that delay is a major cause of death. Saw-scaled vipers and Russell’s viper are the classic examples.

The combined score

The combined score is Venom Hazard and Human Risk averaged with equal weight. It gives one sort order for people who want a single list.

Use it with care. It blends the two questions we separated, so a middling combined score can hide very potent venom on a snake that people rarely meet, or the reverse. On every page we show it next to its two parts.

How far to trust a number

Published figures are often copied from list to list with the original study lost along the way. So every value on this site carries its source and a grade.

GradeWhat it meansHow we use it
AA named, peer-reviewed study with its method statedThe gold standard
BAn expert database, WHO document, or a study reached through oneAccepted
CA secondary compilation that doesn’t restate the methodFine for comparison; flagged
DNo traceable sourceShown with a warning until a source is found

A species’ data grade is the worse of its LD50 grade and its yield grade. Wikipedia tables and “most venomous” rankings are not accepted as sources at all.

Flags

A species is flagged when its sources differ by more than 1.5×, when its LD50 was not tested under the skin, or when an input is grade D.

Why this matters: the hook-nosed sea snake

Three LD50 figures circulate for this species, and they differ eightfold.

We use the 0.164 figure. The other two stay on record, and the species is flagged because its sources disagree. Switching dropped its Venom Hazard from 82 to 69.

Other measures toxinologists use

ED50The dose of antivenom that protects half the animals from a set amount of venom. Used to test antivenoms.
MHD / MNDMinimum haemorrhagic or necrotic dose: the smallest dose that causes a set amount of bleeding or tissue death. More relevant for many viper venoms.
Lab-dish testsClotting, enzyme and cell tests that measure what a venom does without killing animals.
VenomicsIdentifying every protein in a venom by mass spectrometry. It shows how a venom works, not only how lethal it is.
